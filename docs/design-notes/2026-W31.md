## The problem

Say you're checking whether a "near-match" brand color is actually close enough to the original — a rebrand tweak, a vendor's Pantone-to-hex conversion, a dark-mode tint that's supposed to read as "the same blue, just dimmer." You run both through a color-difference calculator and get a Delta E. But depending on which formula the tool uses, the same pair of colors can report a value that's noticeably different — sometimes by 2x. If you don't know which formula you're looking at, you can't tell whether "Delta E: 4.1" means "ship it" or "redo it."

## Why the numbers disagree

There isn't one "Delta E." The name refers to a family of formulas that all measure distance between two colors in CIE Lab space, but they don't measure it the same way.

**CIE76** is the original, simplest version: plain Euclidean distance between two Lab coordinates — `sqrt(ΔL² + Δa² + Δb²)`. It's fast and easy to implement, but Lab space isn't perceptually uniform: equal distances in Lab don't correspond to equal perceived differences. CIE76 in particular overstates differences in saturated colors and understates them in some other regions, because it treats the Lab space as if it were a plain sphere when human color perception isn't.

**CIEDE2000** (from Sharma, Wu & Dalal, 2005) corrects for this. It weights lightness, chroma, and hue separately, adds a hue-rotation term for the blue region, and adjusts for how sensitivity to chroma differences changes with saturation. It's the current standard for accessibility and print-matching work specifically because it tracks human judgment more closely — but it's also more expensive to compute and harder to reason about by hand, which is why plenty of tools still default to CIE76.

The two formulas are not off by a fixed ratio — the gap moves around depending on where in the gamut your two colors sit. That's the actual trap: you can't "convert" a CIE76 number into a CIEDE2000 number with a multiplier. You have to know which one you're reading.

## What to do about it

- **Don't trust a bare "Delta E" number without the formula name attached.** If a tool, a design spec, or a vendor deliverable says "Delta E: 3.2" with no formula named, ask which one — or better, get both.
- **Use CIEDE2000 for anything perceptual** — brand-color tolerance checks, "is this the same color across two screens" QA, print-to-screen matching. It's the one built to match how people actually perceive differences.
- **Rule-of-thumb thresholds for CIEDE2000** (not a formal standard, but a useful working scale): under 1 is imperceptible to most people; 1–2 shows up only in close side-by-side comparison; 2–10 reads as a visible shade difference within the same color family; above 10 reads as a genuinely different color.
- **When you set a tolerance for a design system or QA gate, write down which formula it's measured in.** A CIEDE2000 threshold of 2.0 and a CIE76 threshold of 2.0 are not the same bar — treating them interchangeably is how "passing" swatches slip through that actually look different.

You can compare both formulas side by side, with the plain-language read included, on the [color comparison tool](https://colorarchive.org/compare/).
